How the depth is estimated

An OPG flattens the whole bucco-lingual depth of the jaw into one pixel, so that depth can't be read back from the image. PanoSlice wraps the OPG around an average adult arch, condyle to condyle. Each column becomes a point along the arch and each row becomes a height.

At each point it grows a cross-section. How wide it grows depends on how dense the OPG is there, since the brightness of a pixel roughly tracks how much tooth or bone the X-ray passed through. Teeth are separated from bone by local contrast inside the tooth-bearing band. Average incisor inclination, chin shape and gum contours come from typical adult anatomy.

Millimetre values assume the OPG has the same magnification everywhere. Real panoramic machines don't, so treat every distance as approximate.

What you get is a plausible model, not this patient's anatomy. Ghost images, the spine shadow and positioning errors get wrapped in as well. Use it for teaching and demos. For real 3D, take a CBCT.

About and credits

PanoSlice is an educational demo, not a medical device. Don’t use it for diagnosis, treatment planning or anything a patient relies on.

Teeth are found by a small U-Net trained on 116 panoramic radiographs from Abdi, Kasaei and Mehdizadeh (J. Med. Imaging, 2015), with tooth masks by S. Helli and A. Hamamcı (2022). That data is licensed for non-commercial research, and so is this model.

Depth, gums and bone shape come from average adult anatomy, not from the image.

Everything runs in your browser. Images and DICOM headers are never uploaded, and patient details in DICOM files are not read.
